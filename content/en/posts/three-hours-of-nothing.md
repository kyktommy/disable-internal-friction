It's 11pm. You're not tired yet. You pick up your phone. A YouTube video — eight minutes, nothing serious. You check the score of the game. You flip to Slack, scroll through a few work threads. You open another tab. Another video. The game's at halftime so you watch the highlights.

You look up. It's 2am.

Three hours. Gone. And you can't name a single thing you got from them.

---

## The Drift State

This isn't laziness. Laziness has a certain honesty to it — you chose to do nothing, and you're at peace with it.

This is something else. You were busy the whole time. Your fingers were moving, your eyes were scanning, your brain was processing. You just weren't choosing any of it. Each tap led to the next tap. Each clip led to the next clip. You weren't resting and you weren't working. You were drifting.

Drift looks productive on the surface. You're "catching up on things." You're "staying informed." You're "winding down." But pay attention to how you feel at 2am. Not recharged. Not satisfied. Slightly hollow, slightly anxious, and now annoyed that you have to sleep.

The screens gave you stimulation without satisfaction. Motion without direction.

---

## Why You Don't Stop

Here's the mechanics of it. Each activity is small enough to feel harmless. One video is five minutes. Checking the score takes thirty seconds. Skimming a Slack thread — a minute, tops.

None of these individually feel like a commitment. So you never hit a decision point. There's no moment where you sit down and say "I'm going to spend three hours doing this." The time leaks out in micro-increments, and each increment feels too small to worry about.

Your brain is also getting intermittent rewards. A funny clip. A notification. The score updates. These are tiny dopamine hits — just enough to keep you clicking, never enough to leave you fulfilled. Slot machine mechanics, running on your phone at midnight.

And underneath it all, there's a quieter force. You don't stop because stopping means choosing. Choosing what to do with the time. Choosing to go to sleep. Choosing to work on the thing you've been avoiding. Drift is how you postpone that moment of decision indefinitely.

---

## The Cost You Don't See

Lost sleep. That's the obvious one. But there's a subtler cost.

Every evening that dissolves into drift reinforces a story: *my time isn't really mine.* You start treating your own hours as something that just happens to you, like weather. Monday disappears. Tuesday disappears. By Friday, a quiet resentment builds — you're annoyed at the week, at the screens, at yourself. But the resentment is misdirected. The screens didn't steal your time. You handed it over, three minutes at a time.

The real damage is to your sense of agency. You wake up the next morning feeling like you're already behind. You carry a low-grade guilt that you can't quite name. And tonight, the same pattern runs again — partly because you're tired from last night's late drift, partly because the guilt makes you want to numb out, and the cycle tightens.

---

## One Small Barrier Changes Everything

You don't need a productivity system. You don't need to block apps or set timers. You need one thing: a moment of pause before the drift begins.

At 11pm, before you pick up the phone, ask yourself one question: *What do I actually want from the next hour?*

Not what you should do. What you want. Maybe you want to watch something — great, pick a specific show and watch it. Maybe you want to read. Maybe you want to go to bed early for once. Maybe you want to sit in silence for fifteen minutes.

The answer doesn't matter as much as the asking. Because the question forces a choice. And a choice — any choice — breaks the drift.

The evenings that disappear are the ones where you never decided to begin them. A single moment of intention changes the entire shape of the night.

## What Adlerian Psychology Says

Adler would look at the drift and ask: what is it doing for you?

This is uncomfortable. You want to say the drift is pointless. Adler would disagree. Every behavior serves a purpose, even when the behavior looks like nothing. The late-night screen loop protects you from something. Maybe from confronting what you'd do with the time if you actually owned it. Maybe from the vulnerability of trying something and finding out you're not good at it. Maybe from the silence of sitting with yourself.

This is Adler's teleology at work. You don't drift because you lack discipline. You drift because drifting is safe. It keeps you occupied without requiring you to expose yourself. No risk of failure. No risk of judgment. No risk of discovering what you actually want — which can be the most unsettling discovery of all.

Adler's separation of tasks applies here too. The question "what should I be doing at 11pm?" is often someone else's question — your boss's, society's, some idealized version of yourself. Adler would say: put that down. Ask instead: "What is *my* task right now?" If your task is to rest, rest fully. If your task is to create something, create something. The friction comes from hovering between tasks that aren't yours and avoiding the one that is.

## What the Diamond Sutra and Heart Sutra Say

The Heart Sutra says: 「色即是空，空即是色」 — "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form." Those three hours felt full — images, sounds, notifications, movement. They were empty. No presence, no intention, no real contact with what was happening. The form of activity concealed the emptiness of engagement.

The Diamond Sutra teaches 「應無所住而生其心」 — "Give rise to the mind that dwells nowhere." Drift is a mind that dwells everywhere and nowhere — jumping from screen to screen, topic to topic, never landing. The sutra's instruction points to a different kind of freedom. Let go of clinging to stimulation, and the mind naturally settles. You don't need to force focus. You need to stop feeding the restlessness.

When you sit at 11pm and the pull toward the phone arises, that pull is form. It feels solid, urgent, automatic. The sutras would remind you: it has no fixed essence. You can watch it rise without obeying it. The space between the impulse and the action — that space is where your evening lives or dies.

## What the I Ching Says

**Hexagram 4: Méng (蒙) — Youthful Folly**

Méng's image is a spring emerging at the foot of a mountain — water that has just begun to flow and doesn't yet know its course. The judgment reads: 「蒙，亨。匪我求童蒙，童蒙求我」 — "Youthful folly has success. I do not seek the young fool; the young fool seeks me." The wisdom doesn't chase you. You have to turn toward it.

This is the evening drift: a spring flowing without direction. The water isn't bad. The energy is there. But without a channel, it spreads thin and soaks into the ground, accomplishing nothing. The Xiangzhuan says: 「山下出泉，蒙；君子以果行育德」 — "A spring emerges below the mountain: the image of youth. The noble one acts decisively to cultivate virtue." Decisive action — 果行 — is the channel that gives the spring its power.

Méng doesn't ask you to become a different person. It asks you to give the water a direction. One choice at 11pm. One channel for the spring. That's the difference between three hours that vanish and three hours that remain.

## Try This

Tonight at 11pm, before you touch any screen, say out loud what you want to do for the next hour. One sentence. "I'm going to watch two episodes of this show." "I'm going to bed." "I'm going to read for thirty minutes." Then do exactly that. The point isn't what you pick. The point is that you picked.
