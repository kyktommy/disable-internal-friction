Have you ever done the most work on a project, only to watch someone else take the credit?

What rises in your chest isn't just frustration — it's a deep sense of being erased. "I did all of that, and nobody even noticed."

Then you start keeping score: what I did, what they did, what the boss saw. You think you're fighting for fairness. In reality, you're being consumed by a game you can never win.

---

## Chasing Credit Is an Endless Drain

Let's face the uncomfortable truth: in most organizations, credit has never been distributed fairly.

- People who talk well get seen more than people who work well
- Whoever presents at the end often takes the biggest spotlight
- Your manager's memory is selective — and it doesn't always select you
- Some people are simply better at managing their image

This isn't your fault. But it is reality. And when you pour your energy into "making sure my contributions are visible," you're doing something deeply exhausting:

**You're trying to control other people's perceptions.**

How others see you, how your boss allocates credit, whether your colleague is quietly taking your work — none of this is in your control. The more you try to control it, the more anxious you become. The more anxious you become, the more drained you feel.

---

## What You Actually Take Away Never Needs Anyone's Confirmation

What deserves your attention is not whose name gets called out, but what you genuinely carry away from your work — the kind of gains that don't evaporate the moment someone else takes the microphone:

- **Skills you accumulate** — every project you complete, no matter whose name ends up on the summary, adds experience that lives permanently in you
- **Tangible compensation** — salary, bonuses, promotion opportunities — these are written into contracts, not into someone else's opinion of you
- **Trust and relationships** — the people who actually worked alongside you know exactly what you bring to the table. That recognition runs far deeper than a passing mention in a meeting
- **Inner steadiness** — you know what you did and what you gave. That self-knowledge requires no external endorsement, and it doesn't shake easily

Credit is fluid — it depends on who is speaking, who is listening, and who happens to be in a generous mood that day. But the competence and experience you walk away with have settled into you for good. **What truly belongs to you was never something you needed to fight for.**

---

## Why You Care So Much About Being Seen

If you're honest with yourself, the urge to chase credit usually masks a deeper need:

**You need external validation to feel like you matter.**

There's no shame in this — almost everyone carries this need. But the problem is, when you outsource your sense of worth, you hand over the remote control.

- Your boss praises you? Today you feel worthy.
- Someone takes your credit? You feel invisible.
- A colleague gets recognized? You start wondering if you're not good enough.

Your emotional state is entirely at the mercy of other people's reactions. This isn't working — this is **spinning inside an anxiety loop, waiting to be recognized**.

---

## Letting Go of Credit Doesn't Mean Being a Pushover

Some people hear "stop chasing credit" and think: "So I should just let people walk all over me?"

No. Letting go of credit means **redirecting your energy from what's hollow to what's real**.

### 1. Invest in Building Strength, Not Managing Image

Instead of spending mental energy making sure your boss notices your contribution, put that energy into actually getting better. Once your capability reaches a certain level, it speaks for itself.

### 2. Let Your Output Do the Talking

You don't need to rush to claim credit. Consistently deliver high-quality results, and time will sort out the rest. The people who actually care about quality will notice.

### 3. Protect Your Boundaries

Not chasing credit doesn't mean tolerating theft. If someone deliberately misrepresents your work or takes credit in bad faith, absolutely push back. But push back to protect your boundary — not to win a scoreboard battle.

### 4. Focus on What You Gained

After every project, ask yourself: What did I learn from this? Did my skills grow? Did I build new relationships? If the answer is yes, then who got the credit is genuinely irrelevant.

## What Adlerian Psychology Says

Adler would ask you one blunt question: **What is chasing credit actually doing for you?**

According to Adlerian teleology, your obsession with credit isn't because "credit was unfairly taken." It's because **the act of chasing credit is serving a psychological purpose**:

- Maybe it's a **craving for belonging**: you need to be seen to feel like you're part of the team
- Maybe it's **compensation for inferiority**: credit is the tool you use to prove "I'm good enough"
- Maybe it's **avoiding a deeper insecurity**: as long as you're busy fighting for recognition, you don't have to face the possibility that "even if I were recognized, I'd still feel insufficient"

Adler's separation of tasks is especially powerful here: **what you do is your task. How others evaluate it is their task.**

You can't control your boss's attention, your colleague's words, or your organization's culture. But you can control what you invest in, what you build, and what you take with you.

Adler would also remind you of **social interest**: true fulfillment doesn't come from individual praise — it comes from knowing you contributed something meaningful to the group. When your motivation shifts from "I need to be seen" to "I want to create something valuable," you stop needing applause to confirm your existence.

And the **courage to be imperfect** tells you this: you don't need to be recognized every time, and you don't need every piece of credit to land on you. Do the work worth doing. Take the pay worth taking. Build the skills worth building. That's enough.

## What the Diamond Sutra and Heart Sutra Say

The Diamond Sutra teaches the Bodhisattva's practice of giving: *"A Bodhisattva who gives a gift should not be attached to anything — not to form, not to sound, smell, taste, touch, or idea. Giving without attachment produces immeasurable merit."* This is not advice for monks only. It is the precise antidote to credit-chasing. When you work while attached to how your contribution will be received, your work itself becomes distorted — shaped by the need for recognition rather than by the work's own requirements. Give your effort, your skill, your time — without grasping for the return. The merit accrues regardless.

## What the I Ching Says

Hexagram Qiān (謙, Humility, Hexagram 15) is the mountain beneath the earth — extraordinary capability concealed in an unpretentious form. It is one of the only hexagrams in the I Ching where every single line brings good fortune. The Confucian observation: *"The way of heaven diminishes the full and augments the humble."* Those who stop chasing recognition and simply work with excellence do not lose — they accumulate. And when the right moment comes, the mountain reveals itself. You do not need to announce it.

---

Credit is given by others, and it can be taken back at any time.

But the skills you've built, the experience you've gained, the quiet knowledge of what you actually did — that's yours. Forever.

Stop chasing credit. Just make sure you get paid.

Save your energy for the work that matters — not for applause that may never come.
