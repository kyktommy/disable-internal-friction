You're at home on a Friday night. It's quiet. You're reading something, or cooking, or just sitting. It's fine. It was fine — until you opened your phone.

Someone's at a rooftop party. Someone else just landed in Tokyo. Your old classmate posted about her startup's Series A. A guy you barely know is running his third marathon.

Now your quiet Friday feels like a failure. The book is still in your hand, but you're not reading anymore. You're somewhere else — scattered across a dozen lives that aren't yours, calculating what you should be doing instead.

Nothing about your evening changed. Your attention just left the room.

---

## The Real Cost Isn't What You Missed

FOMO feels like it's about experiences. That trip you didn't take. That event you skipped. That career path you didn't choose.

Look closer. You didn't actually lose those things — most of them were never yours to begin with. What you lost is something you did have: the ability to be where you are.

Every minute spent mentally attending a party you're not at is a minute subtracted from the evening you're actually in. Every hour spent comparing your trajectory to someone else's is an hour you could have spent moving along your own. The fear of missing out makes you miss what's already here.

That's the friction. You're running on a treadmill inside your own head, sprinting toward everywhere, arriving nowhere.

---

## You're Not Afraid of Missing Out — You're Afraid of Choosing Wrong

Strip FOMO down to its skeleton and something specific is underneath: the terror of irreversibility.

Every choice closes a door. Saying yes to this city means saying no to that one. Spending your evening here means not spending it there. Committing to this career means the other one fades a little further.

That's not a problem. That's how time works. You get one evening, one Friday, one life unfolding in one direction. The pain isn't in the limitation — it's in the refusal to accept it.

So you keep every door mentally propped open. You check what's behind the ones you didn't walk through. You maintain a phantom portfolio of alternate lives — just in case, just to compare, just to make sure you didn't blow it.

Maintaining that portfolio is exhausting. And it never reassures you. It just adds more doors to monitor.

---

## The Scroll That Never Ends

Social media didn't invent FOMO. But it gave it an IV drip.

Before phones, your Friday night was just your Friday night. You might hear on Monday that someone had a great weekend. You'd think, "Oh, nice," and move on. The information arrived after the moment had passed. It couldn't hijack your experience in real time.

Now every moment comes with a live scoreboard of what everyone else is doing. The feed is infinite. The comparisons are instant. And the algorithm is optimized to show you the highlights — the best angle, the peak moment, the version of someone's life that makes your own feel small.

You're comparing your unfiltered interior to everyone else's curated exterior. That comparison will never come out in your favor. It can't. It was never a fair game.

---

## The Life You Already Chose

Here's what FOMO obscures: you already chose. You're already living a life. It has a shape, a texture, a set of people and places and rhythms that belong to it. Every life has gaps — things it doesn't include. That's what makes it a specific life and not an abstract idea.

The question isn't "Am I missing out?" You are. Everyone is. Right now, there are thousands of beautiful things happening that you will never see. That has always been true. It was true before smartphones. It was true before electricity. It was true for every human being who has ever lived.

The question is: can you stay in the life you're actually living long enough to experience it?

Because that's what FOMO steals. Not opportunities — attention. And attention is the only thing that turns a moment into an experience. Without it, you could be at that rooftop party and still feel like you're missing out on something else.

## What Adlerian Psychology Says

Adler would look at FOMO and ask a blunt question: what purpose does it serve you?

This is **teleological thinking** (目的論) — the idea that your behavior always serves a present goal, even when it feels involuntary. Scrolling through other people's lives at 11 p.m. isn't something that just "happens to you." It's doing something for you. Maybe it lets you avoid evaluating your own choices directly. Maybe it provides a comfortable excuse: "I can't feel satisfied — look at everything I'm missing." As long as the gap between your life and some imagined better life stays open, you never have to commit fully to the one you have.

Adler's concept of **separation of tasks** (課題分離) cuts through the comparison trap. Other people's choices, careers, vacations, milestones — those are their tasks. Your task is your life. When you measure your evening against someone else's evening, you've crossed into a domain that was never yours. No amount of monitoring their task will advance yours.

There's also the matter of **community feeling** (共同體感覺). FOMO positions other people as competitors in a race for the best life. Adler saw it differently — belonging comes from contribution, not comparison. When you stop tracking who's ahead, you can start noticing who's beside you.

## What the Diamond Sutra and Heart Sutra Say

The Diamond Sutra teaches: 「凡所有相，皆是虛妄」 — "All appearances are illusory." That rooftop party photo is an appearance. That career milestone post is an appearance. The version of someone's life that triggers your FOMO is a form — a curated slice, a frozen frame. You're generating suffering from an illusion, then comparing it against the full, unedited weight of your own lived experience.

The sutra also says: 「應無所住而生其心」 — "Give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere." FOMO is a mind that dwells everywhere except here. It's lodged in Tokyo, at the marathon, inside someone else's startup, at the party you didn't attend. The teaching isn't to suppress those images. It's to stop parking your mind there. Let them pass through like any other thought — arriving, dissolving, gone.

The Heart Sutra declares: 「心無罣礙，無罣礙故，無有恐怖」 — "The mind without obstruction — because there is no obstruction, there is no fear." FOMO is a kind of fear. Fear that your choices were wrong. Fear that life is happening somewhere you're not. Remove the obstruction — the compulsive clinging to lives you're not living — and the fear has nothing left to feed on.

## What the I Ching Says

**Hexagram 52: Gen — Keeping Still (Mountain)**

Gen is mountain doubled — mountain above, mountain below. A mountain does not chase the horizon. It does not lean toward the next valley. It rests in its own place, fully formed, fully present.

The judgment reads: 「艮其背，不獲其身。行其庭，不見其人。無咎。」 — "Keeping still his back, he does not feel his body. Walking in his courtyard, he does not see the people. No blame." The image is vivid: when stillness penetrates your back — your blind spot, the place you can't watch — you stop being aware of yourself as a performer. Walking through your own courtyard, you stop comparing who's there with who isn't. No blame. Nothing missing.

The *Xiangzhuan* says: 「兼山，艮。君子以思不出其位。」 — "Mountains standing together: the image of Keeping Still. The superior person's thoughts do not go beyond their position." Your position is where you are right now — this evening, this room, this life. The I Ching doesn't say your position is the best position. It says your thoughts don't need to leave it. When you stop sending your mind to every other mountain on the horizon, the one you're standing on finally becomes solid beneath your feet.

## Try This

Tonight, before you open any social app, pause. Ask yourself: "Am I looking for something, or am I running from where I already am?" If the answer is the second one, put the phone down and stay in your evening for ten more minutes. Notice what it actually feels like when your attention isn't split across other people's lives.
