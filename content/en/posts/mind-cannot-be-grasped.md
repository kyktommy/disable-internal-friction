Have you ever spent an entire afternoon replaying something you said wrong yesterday?

You revised the wording, imagined a better response, mentally re-staged the whole scene — as if thinking about it enough could transport you back to that moment and fix it.

Then you snapped out of it. Half the day was gone. Nothing got done. The words were still said. The only thing that changed was that you'd burned through half a day's worth of energy.

Or the reverse: you haven't even started something, and you're already worried it'll fail. You've imagined the worst outcome, rehearsed the awkward aftermath, pre-experienced the disappointment. By the time you finally sit down to do the thing, you're exhausted — drained by something that hasn't happened.

This is internal friction in its most invisible form: **you're chasing a thought that's already gone, or fighting a thought that doesn't yet exist.**

---

## Your Mind Never Stays

Thoughts have a quality we rarely face directly: **they never stay.**

Pay attention: is your mind still on the same thing it was a moment ago? Probably not. It's already moved to something related. Then something related to that. Each thought is like running water. You reach in, and all you're left with is wet hands.

That past thought? It's gone. The "memory" in your head right now is actually a brand-new thought generated in this moment, filtered through today's emotions. When you replay yesterday's conversation, what you feel isn't yesterday's mood — it's today's regret.

That future thought? It doesn't exist yet. The "pressure of tomorrow" is really today's anxiety dressed up as a forecast. The "preparing" is just suffering in advance.

And the present thought? Try to grab "right now." By the time you register "I'm experiencing this moment," the moment has already passed.

All three directions. None of them can be held.

---

## The Friction Comes From the Gripping Itself

Here's the key: **what exhausts you isn't the thought itself — it's the act of trying to hold onto it.**

Thoughts come and go effortlessly — like breathing, like clouds crossing the sky. You don't need to manage them. They flow through on their own.

But we do something extra. We reach out and grab. We grab a past thought and turn it into rumination. We grab a future thought and turn it into anxiety. We even grab the present thought, demanding that we "stay focused" or "live in the moment" — then blame ourselves when we can't.

Every grab is a moment of internal friction. Your system is attempting something impossible: fixing in place what is inherently flowing. It's like squeezing running water in your fist. The tighter you grip, the faster it escapes, the more tired you become.

---

## Letting Go Isn't Losing — It's Stopping Something Unnecessary

"Letting go" sounds like giving something up. Look closer — you never actually had it.

You never possessed yesterday's thought. You can't pre-own tomorrow's. You can't even hold onto this moment's thought for more than a second.

Letting go simply means stopping the pretense that you were holding anything in the first place.

The experience is the opposite of a loss: **when you stop chasing thoughts, the energy drain stops.** No new technique required. Just stop doing something old — stop gripping what was always leaving anyway.

---

## Lightness Isn't a Destination

"Relaxation" and "peace" sound like destinations. Do the right thing, and you earn a moment of ease.

But if thoughts can't be grasped in the first place, then you were already light — you just couldn't feel it because you kept making an unnecessary effort.

Imagine you've been holding your fists clenched and raised in the air. Someone asks: "How can you relax?" The answer isn't to find a relaxation technique. The answer is: **put your hands down.**

Your internal friction, much of the time, is a pair of invisible clenched fists.

## What Adlerian Psychology Says

Adler made a foundational claim: human behavior is purpose-driven (teleological). When you replay a past failure over and over, it's not because the past "caused" your suffering. It's because present-you has chosen to use that memory to serve a purpose. Maybe self-protection: "If I keep remembering this lesson, I won't get hurt again." Maybe avoidance: "If I'm still processing the past, I don't have to face the present."

This lens makes the "three ungrasped minds" very practical. You're clinging to a past thought not because the past controls you, but because the act of clinging is doing something for you right now.

Adler's concept of **separation of tasks** applies here too: what already happened belongs to the past's domain; what hasn't happened yet belongs to the future's. The only thing you can actually respond to is what's in front of you right now. When you redirect your energy from chasing ungrasped minds back to the one thing you can act on, the internal drain naturally subsides.

Admitting "I can't hold onto this" takes what Adler called the **courage to be imperfect**. Releasing the illusion of control means accepting that you can't fix the past or prevent every future risk. This isn't weakness — it's honesty about the nature of how life flows.

## What the Diamond Sutra and Heart Sutra Say

"The past mind cannot be grasped, the present mind cannot be grasped, the future mind cannot be grasped."

This line from the Diamond Sutra points to something you experience every single day. Recall what you felt five minutes ago — it's already different. Predict how you'll feel tomorrow — that's just a projection made by today's mood. Pin down "my current state" — by the time you've defined it, it's shifted.

The three minds cannot be grasped means: **there is no fixed "mind" you can catch, define, or own.**

Unsettling? Flip it around. If a thought could truly be grasped, you'd truly be trapped by it. Precisely because it is "ungrasped" — inherently flowing, inherently empty — you have the possibility of not being hijacked by it. That's the liberation.

The Heart Sutra says: "The mind has no obstruction. Because there is no obstruction, there is no fear. Far beyond all inverted thinking." The obstruction is the grabbing itself. When you see thoughts arriving and departing, arriving and departing, and you don't need to chase any of them, the mind is naturally unobstructed. Without obstruction, there is no fear. Without fear, all that tortured "inverted thinking" — the past you believe you must resolve, the future you believe you must guard against — gradually quiets down.

"Produce a mind that dwells nowhere" — let the mind keep working, keep feeling, keep responding. Don't nail it to any single place.

## What the I Ching Says

**Hexagram 4: Meng — Youthful Folly**

Meng has Gen (mountain) above and Kan (water) below. A spring emerges beneath the mountain, its direction still unclear — like a restless mind groping between past and future, unsure which way to go.

The judgment reads: *"Youthful Folly brings success. It is not I who seeks the young fool — the young fool seeks me. At the first oracle I inform. If asked two or three times, it is profanation. If profaned, I give no information."*

Uncertainty can lead to clarity — bring your question sincerely, once, and that's enough. Asking again and again is "profanation." The repeated asking is a way of avoiding the answer that has already appeared.

This is exactly what "the three minds cannot be grasped" looks like in daily life. When you replay the past over and over, you're casting the oracle a second and third time — you're not looking for an answer, you're running from the one that already surfaced. Worrying about the future repeatedly is the same profanation. One more round of thinking won't make you safer. Each round takes you further from clarity.

The *Tuanzhuan* commentary says: *"To nourish correctness in a state of youth — this is the work of the sage."* The state of not-knowing isn't the problem. Cultivating right attention within uncertainty is the real practice. You don't need to see every direction before you can take a step. You just need to stop asking the same unanswerable question over and over.

The *Xiangzhuan* says: *"A spring emerges beneath the mountain — youthful folly. The superior person nurtures virtue through decisive action."* The spring starts out muddy. It keeps flowing, and gradually runs clear. Decisive action means staying in motion. The thoughts clear up along the way.

To stop chasing the three minds is to let the spring find its own course.

## Try This

The next time you catch yourself replaying something that already happened, or rehearsing something that hasn't — try saying to yourself: "That thought is already gone. I'm chasing something that isn't here." You don't need to stop the thought from appearing. Just notice that you're reaching for it, and let your hand open. Pay attention to what your body feels like once you do.
