There is a moment most of us know well. A task lands in your inbox. A problem surfaces at work. A conversation goes sideways. And before you even start dealing with it, you are already exhausted — not by the thing itself, but by the mental weight of resisting it.

That resistance is internal friction. And it is costing you more than you think.

---

## The Hidden Tax of Resistance

Internal friction is not laziness. It is not lack of motivation. It is the energy burned in the gap between where you are and where you think you should not have to be.

When you frame something as a burden — "why is this happening to me," "this is unfair," "I hate this kind of thing" — you are not solving the problem. You are fighting the fact that the problem exists. That fight is a tax on your energy, and it pays nothing back.

The task is still there. The conversation still needs to happen. The deadline still approaches. But now you are arriving at it depleted.

---

## The Shift: From Burden to Challenge

A challenge is the same situation, framed differently.

A burden says: *I have to do this.*
A challenge says: *Can I figure this out?*

A burden drains before it begins.
A challenge carries its own energy — the quiet pull of curiosity, problem-solving, and forward motion.

This is not toxic positivity. You are not pretending the hard thing is easy. You are simply refusing to spend energy fighting the reality of it before you even engage.

---

## What Makes Something Feel Like a Drain

Most internal friction comes from three sources:

**1. Unmet expectations**
You expected the meeting to go smoothly. It did not. The friction is not the difficult meeting — it is the collision between what you expected and what happened. The moment you release the expectation, the friction often dissolves.

**2. Loss of perceived control**
When circumstances feel imposed on you, resistance rises. Reframing as a challenge restores a sense of agency: you may not control what happened, but you can control how you approach it.

**3. Identity threat**
Some problems feel like proof of inadequacy. "I should already know how to handle this." That story turns a solvable problem into a referendum on your worth. A challenge frame separates the problem from the person: the problem is out there, and you are a capable person looking at it.

---

## Practicing the Reframe

This is not a one-time mindset shift. It is a practice — something you return to each time the friction starts.

A few ways to make it concrete:

**Name what you are resisting.** Often the friction is not about the task at all. It is about something the task represents: being judged, being wrong, losing time, losing face. Name that. It loses power when you see it clearly.

**Ask "what would make this interesting?"** Not every task is inherently engaging. But almost any task has a dimension that can pull you in — a small skill to improve, a person to understand better, a constraint that forces creativity. Find the angle.

**Shrink the scope until it feels like a game.** The full problem may feel overwhelming. But the next ten minutes? That is manageable. Make it a challenge to simply begin.

**Track small wins.** Challenges have outcomes. Burdens just accumulate. When you keep score — even loosely — you build evidence that your effort moves things forward. That evidence makes the next challenge easier to accept.

---

## The Deeper Reason This Matters

Internal friction is cumulative. A day spent resisting things is exhausting in a way that a day of hard work is not. The exhaustion from resistance is hollow — it produces nothing and leaves you with less.

A life where most things feel like challenges is not a life free of difficulty. It is a life where difficulty has a direction. Where effort feels purposeful. Where you arrive at the end of a hard day tired in the good way — because you were in the game, not fighting the field.

The problems are not going away. But the drain does not have to come with them.

See it as a challenge. Start there.
